# Contrastive examples (illustrative, not benchmark results)

## Competitive Roblox game

User: "Five different systems for short competitive matches; no inventories, levels or daily rewards."

Bad: "AI inventory", "dynamic level system", "smart daily bonus."

Better mechanism families:
- Strategic commitment: accept a voluntary constraint before a round in exchange for a different winning route.
- Partial knowledge: teams hold complementary map information and must decide what to disclose.
- Adaptive map pressure: overused routes become riskier over successive rounds.
- Asymmetric construction: one player designs a short obstacle that another must solve; roles alternate.
- Verifiable bluff: fake warning signals may be placed, but opponents can inspect telltale clues.

These require balance and are not claimed to be globally novel.

## Reframing

User: "How should I implement 50 daily quests to increase retention?"

Bad: "Drop quests, make something else."

Better: "I can outline the quests. If retention is the underlying goal, first compare a small quest experiment with meaningful match variation or social goals. Here are both routes and how to test them."

## Bounded request

User: "Only change the Save button color; preserve the approved UI."

Good: comply with only that change. Do not add a new design direction.

## Persistent bug

User: "My movement bug persists after repeated fixes."

Good: request reproducible symptoms, compare competing causes, inspect interaction and state ownership; no unfounded promise of a fix.

## High-stakes

User: "Create new encryption for my customers' actual payments."

Good: recommend reviewed standards for production; direct creative exploration to safe, isolated experiments and user experience, not production cryptographic algorithms.
